Mathematical discussion and conclusions

Riemann vs Lebesgue

For Riemann-integrable functions, the Riemann and Lebesgue integrals agree. The numerical methods may differ in speed and accuracy: midpoint quadrature is efficient on smooth functions, whereas range quantization introduces a bin-width error and requires estimating preimage measures.

Dirichlet: the indicator of the rationals is Lebesgue-integrable with integral 0, but is not Riemann-integrable. Thomae: f(p/q)=1/q in lowest terms and zero at irrationals is Riemann- and Lebesgue-integrable with integral 0. Rational computer grids cannot establish these facts: the implementation intentionally demonstrates sampling bias. 1/√x: unbounded near zero, but both the improper Riemann integral and Lebesgue integral equal 2.

Normal CDF and Liouville's theorem

The Gaussian antiderivative cannot be written in elementary functions. Liouville's theorem on elementary antiderivatives provides a rigorous criterion: the equation R'(x)−2xR(x)=1 has no rational-function solution R, ruling out an elementary primitive for exp(−x²). Introducing the special function erf gives an exact representation, but computers still approximate erf or the integral numerically.

erf(x) = (2/√π) ∫[0,x] exp(−t²) dt
erf(x) = (2/√π) Σₙ≥₀ (−1)ⁿ x^(2n+1)/(n!(2n+1))

Simpson quadrature converges rapidly for the smooth Gaussian density. The power series is accurate near the origin but can lose precision from cancellation for larger arguments. Production libraries typically use piecewise approximations and complementary error functions for tails.
